# The Back Room — Adventure Structure

Oct 22, 2026 · Dan Bularzik. Revised Oct 26, 2026: Pointy Hat method verified and applied (Theme, Mechanics, Goal), Corridor progression rule, encounter mix, Foundry concealment design, the upstairs back room and the library layer. This file in the repo is the source of truth; the Claude doc of the same name is the Oct 22 draft.

## Premise & message

The message in one line: *the places you're most comfortable are the ones you never finished leaving.*

The party meets in a tavern, played completely straight. They take the hook, stock up in the back room, and bed down before the road. They never leave that back room. The whole dungeon — stairwells, stables, corridors, campsites — is the pantry, misremembered. The "adventure" they think they're on is the thing keeping them from the adventure.

The thing in there with them is not a monster. It's the first party: each player's first-ever D&D character, as the table misremembers them — overpowered, cooler than they were, one running joke swollen into a whole personality. These are the **Remnants**. They are not hostile. They want the game to keep going, forever, with the party in it.

Tone curve across the arc:

1. Early rooms are off by one detail (comic, uncanny): the sword in the stone is a spoon.
2. Middle rooms are wrong in ways that implicate the party: a prophecy describing two rooms ago; a barkeep who knows things the characters never said aloud.
3. Late rooms stop pretending. The Remnants are in the room, and they are glad to see you.

**The library layer.** The players at this table began playing D&D in the upstairs rooms of the Morse Institute Library in Natick, Massachusetts: a Gothic Revival reading room with pointed arches, dark timber ribs, two rose-windows of stained glass in arched bays, a stained-glass lantern in the ceiling, green-painted wainscoting, armchairs round a low table, and an inner glass-walled room with double wooden doors and bookshelves (reference photo: `docs/reference/morse-reading-room.jpg`; keep the photo out of the shipped module). That room is the memory the whole adventure is built on, so the dungeon gradually becomes it: one detail in Room 1, one more per Corridor visit, a step per segment in Room 3, and in Room 4 the Hoard *is* the reading room. It is the misremembering, surfacing. For a general audience the shipped text calls it "the upstairs room of a library" and never names the building.

Levels 5–8 is the right tier for this on purpose: past local nobodies, not yet legends. The characters are in a transition whether the players have noticed or not.

## Structure

The spine is Johnn Four's Five-Room Dungeon, but the five rooms are *destinations*, not neighbours: the party reaches each one by wandering the Corridors (a random-door crawl, below). So the shape is five fixed beats threaded through an unfixed maze.

**The Pointy Hat method** (*The Pointy Hat Method of Dungeon Creation*, 2023) is five steps: **Theme** (one word, chosen first), **Mechanics** (express the theme through rules, not description), **Goal** (an objective the players can state), **Encounters** (a deliberate mix of combat, puzzle/trap and roleplay, each exploring the theme from a different angle), **Ending** (a climax that brings theme, mechanics and encounters back together). Steps 3–5 are what the Five-Room Dungeon already provides; steps 1–2 are what it lacks, so they get their own sections below.

| Beat | Five-Room role | Pointy Hat step | This adventure |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Entrance / Guardian | Theme stated; Goal planted | The tavern back room; the barkeep as a gentle gate |
| 2 | Puzzle / Roleplay | Goal made explicit (the X map) | The common room that isn't; find the Remnant among the roles |
| 3 | Trick / Setback | Mechanics turned on the party | The corridor takes something from the party |
| 4 | Climax | Ending: antagonist's motive, mechanics at full strength | The first party, assembled, wants to finish the game |
| 5 | Reward / Revelation / Twist | Ending: theme paid off by a choice | The back room again; open the door or stay |

**Session pacing (2–3 sessions).** Session 1: Rooms 1–2 and two Corridors (visits 1–2). Session 2: three or four Corridors (visits 3–6, including the fight and the glimpse), Room 3. Session 3 (or the back half of 2): a Corridor at visit 7+ with a Remnant in it, Room 4, Room 5. The Corridors are the pacing valve: add loops when the table is enjoying the dread, cut them when it isn't.

**The central gimmick, escalating:** misremembering. Early, the *rooms* are misremembered. Middle, the *party's own past* starts to be. Late, the party meets the misremembered versions of themselves — the Remnants — and has to decide whether to leave them behind.

## Theme

One word: **Unfinished.** Every room is something that was never finished — a story cut off, a character who never leveled, a campaign that died in the spring. "Liminal" describes the dressing; "unfinished" is the point. When choosing a detail for a room, ask what in it was left undone.

## Mechanics

Four rules carry the theme. They go in the players' rules journal, on the sheet, not in GM prose.

1. **Misremembering.** Each short rest taken in a Corridor rewrites one small backstory detail of one character — a sibling's name, the colour of the house, which god. The GM changes it on the sheet and tells only the *other* players. Track every change (who, what it was, what it is now): Room 5 needs the list.
2. **The Signature.** A Remnant's one remembered thing auto-succeeds once per round, no roll, no save. Everything else about it is blank. A finished character has a whole sheet; an unfinished one has one line.
3. **No door leads back.** Every door in the Corridors sends the party somewhere else in the pool, never to the place they left. The one scripted exception is the Room 4 exit. The visit counter (below) escalates what is in there with them.
4. **Leaving something behind is the only way forward.** Each transition out of a fixed room costs something the player chooses — an item set down, a slot spent on nothing, a story told once and never again. It breaks the Room 3 loop, it is what the Remnants are made of, and it is the choice in Room 5. Never gate it on a keyword; reward the first sincere sacrifice the table invents.

## Goal

Two layers, both explicit.

- **The stated goal** arrives in Room 2: the Remnant hands the party a map with an X and says it marks the way out. It points at the Hoard. From then on the players can say what they are doing — follow the X — and the X pulls them through the Corridors.
- **The real goal** is discovered in Rooms 4–5: the way out was the door they came in by. The stated goal was the thing keeping them from it.

**Progression rule (how random doors still lead somewhere).** Every Corridor scene has one door marked with the X — it moves between scenes when nobody looks at the map. The X door leads to the *next fixed room*, but it only appears once the party has done what the previous room required: found the Remnant in Room 2; left something behind in Room 3. All other doors are pool doors. Random doors for wandering, one scripted door for progress.

## Encounters

The mix as originally drafted was five dread encounters and no real fight: Room 1 roleplay, Room 2 puzzle, Room 3 trap, Room 4 a combat that can't be won, Room 5 roleplay. For levels 5–8 over three sessions that is too few dice. Two additions:

- **One honest fight in the Corridors**, at visits 3–6: **The Cover** (below). It is monsters, not Remnants — misremembered ones — and it is the payoff of the "something is in here" escalation before the Remnant appears at visit 7+.
- **The Room 3 echo fight is mandatory**, not conditional on a teleport: the blank-faced copy of one character steps out of the far door on the second loop.

### The Cover (the Corridor fight)

The 1977 AD&D *Monster Manual* cover (David C. Sutherland III) shows a red dragon, a unicorn, a centaur, a troll, a roper and an owlbear, all in one fight with adventurers in a dark, treasure-filled cavern. Monsters with no business being together, and the first treasure hoard these players ever saw.

On the designated Corridor visit, a pool door opens onto that cavern: scene `cover-cavern`, entered once. The six monsters are there, mid-fight, exactly as posed — but the adventurers they are fighting are not. There are gaps in the tableau the shape of people, and a dropped sword where one of them stood. The monsters do not acknowledge the party. They keep fighting nobody, as they have since 1977. (The rule is borrowed from a dryad encounter this table remembers: the thing in the room is busy, and the party has to decide whether to interrupt it.)

**Trigger: stepping into a gap.** Each gap wakes one monster — the one that was fighting whoever stood there — and only that one. The rest keep posing. The party chooses how much of the cover to fight. Two or three is a hard fight for levels 5–8; all six is far beyond deadly, and the GM should let the tableau's stillness say so. Joining the picture is leaving-something-behind in reverse; and the theme, literally: the fight on the cover never ended, and you only have to finish the ones you start.

| Monster | Misremembered as | Build (2024 rules) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Red dragon | The size it was on the cover, about the owlbear's size. Everybody's first party beat a red dragon at level 3, somehow. It never got to grow. | Red dragon wyrmling with a young red dragon's breath weapon |
| Unicorn | On the monsters' side. It was in the Monster Manual, so it is a monster. It is confused about this. | Unicorn; hostile; its healing goes to the other monsters |
| Centaur | Has a bow but no arrows, and shoots anyway. They hit. | Centaur trooper; ranged attacks need no ammunition |
| Troll | Regenerates the way the table remembers, not the way the rules say: it stands up at the start of its turn in the pose it fell in. | Troll; replace Regeneration with the Remnant rule (previewed here) — fire or acid damage ends it |
| Roper | The one nobody could draw properly; it looks like three different things from three angles. | Roper, unchanged; describe it differently each round |
| Owlbear | Just an owlbear. Something in here has to be. | Owlbear, unchanged |

The treasure pile is real and quietly foreshadows Room 4: the Hoard has no dragon because the dragon is here, still frozen. Nothing taken from this pile survives the next Corridor door.

The dryads themselves stay a glimpse cue, not a fight: at the campfire clearing, figures dancing just outside the firelight who never look over.

Corridor #7 (the ten-foot corridor) is cut from the random pool; it *is* Room 3, and only the X door leads into it. Its slot in the pool is taken by the crossroads at dusk.

## Room 1 — The Back Room (Entrance / Guardian)

Play the cliché straight and let the players relax into it. A hooded stranger in the corner booth has a job. The party takes it, buys provisions, and the barkeep leads them **up the stairs** from the common room to the storeroom over the kitchen, where they can sleep before the dawn road. Going upstairs to play is the memory; the back room is on the second floor on purpose. Describe it in loving, mundane detail: a short landing at the top of the stairs, shelves, sacks, a cot per character, one lantern, a small window. In the window, one pane of coloured glass that was not there at supper. Nobody comments on it; the GM does not either.

**The night event (Insomnia Rule).** Someone wakes. The lantern is lit but nobody lit it. The door to the landing now opens onto a stone stairwell going down — the stairs they came up, but wrong: too many steps, and the common room not at the bottom of them. Behind the shelves, a second door nobody noticed: it opens into the **hayloft** over the stable, with a ladder down (this is how the storeroom connects to the yard; it also seeds Corridor #2).

**The first anchor object.** In the corner, a stone with a hilt in it. Whoever pulls draws out a long iron key with no teeth. It opens nothing in the room. Keep it; it matters in Room 5.

**The guardian is not a fight.** The barkeep comes down the stairwell carrying a tray. He is kind. He tells them it's not morning yet and they should go back to bed; the road will be there tomorrow. He knows a detail about one character that the character has never told anyone. If pressed, he doesn't understand the question. The test here is only whether the party chooses the stairwell (or the loft ladder) over the cot. If they go back to sleep, they wake in Room 1 again, and the barkeep has set a second tray.

## Room 2 — The Common Room That Isn't (Puzzle / Roleplay)

A tavern common room, but every patron is a *role*: a hooded stranger in every booth, a quest-giver who repeats the hook word for word, a barmaid who walks in a circle and never arrives at a table, a drunk who says the same line no matter how many times you talk to him. Guards at the door say there's nothing to see here. There's a lot to see here.

**The puzzle.** One patron is not a role. It is the first Remnant: one player's first character, misremembered. It doesn't know it's in a tavern. It thinks it's mid-adventure and the party are old companions. The puzzle is social: figure out which patron is real, and get it to say the thing everyone remembers it for (its Signature). When it does, the room goes quiet and every role turns to look at the party.

**Clues (three, so the table can't miss it).** The Remnant is the only patron who asks the party a question. It's the only one whose drink is a different colour. And the player whose character it is will recognise it from the description before anyone else does — let that player sweat before they say so.

**Payoff.** The Remnant gives them a map with an X. The X moves when nobody looks at it. It's pointing at the Corridors.

## Room 3 — The Corridor Between (Trick / Setback)

The ten-foot corridor with a door at the far end. Two doors, actually: the near one is locked and its key is behind the far one. The far one opens onto the same corridor, from the other end. Torches are lit ahead and dark behind.

**The trick.** Walking forward loops. Each pass is one step more library — ribs overhead, then the green paint, then the pointed arch at the far door — which reads as progress, and is: the corridor is going somewhere. The loop breaks on any sincere act of leaving something behind — a treasured item set down, a spell slot spent on nothing, a story a player tells about their first character and then agrees never to tell again. Don't gate it on a keyword; reward the first real sacrifice the table invents.

**The setback.** Whatever was left behind, a Remnant now has. And the corridor has taken something it wasn't offered: one character's bond, ideal, or a named person from their backstory is now subtly different, and only the other players notice. This is the point where misremembering stops being about the rooms and starts being about the party.

**The echo.** On the second pass through the far door, a blank-faced copy of one character steps out of it (the one who has left the least behind, if there's a choice). In this fight every attack repeats itself one round later in the same squares: the party must fight their own last turn. The echo has the character's sheet with every number halved and no name. It stops when the loop breaks.

## Room 4 — The Hoard (Climax)

A dragon's hoard with no dragon — and the room is the upstairs reading room, fully arrived: pointed arches and dark timber ribs overhead, the stained-glass lantern in the ceiling, two rose-windows with dawn behind them (the same dawn as Room 5), green wainscoting, armchairs. The hoard is the ring of armchairs round the low table, and on the table, where the coins should be, are dice — warm, as if they have just been rolled. The dark lord's tower, painted, hangs between the arches where a portrait would; it is the same size it has been from every window. At the far end, the inner glass-walled room with the double wooden doors: through the glass, bookshelves, and the Remnants, seated, waiting for the session to start.

**The antagonist's motive.** The Remnants are all here, the full first party. They have been waiting for the boss. They think the party *is* the boss, or the rescue, or the sequel — it changes mid-sentence. What they want is simple and sympathetic: for the session to not end. They were never given an ending; they are offering the party the same.

**The fight that can't be won.** Remnants have max HP and their Signature always works. Dropped to 0, a Remnant stands up at the start of its next turn in the same pose it fell in. Beating them rebuilds the room (the load-bearing boss, in reverse). Spell the stakes out at the top of round 3: the players should *understand* that winning is the loop.

**How it actually ends.** The glass double doors the party came in by now lead back the way they came — the first time in the adventure that's been true. Combat ends the moment a character walks through it without looking back. Remnants will not follow. They will call after the party by the players' real character names from Room 1, and then by the *first* characters' names, and then stop.

## Room 5 — The Back Room, Again (Reward / Revelation / Twist)

The storeroom over the kitchen. Dawn light under the landing door, and through the small window — which is now, entirely, a stained-glass window, the colours of the reading room laid across the cots. The cots are made. The lantern is out. The stone in the corner has no hilt in it now, but the toothless key from Room 1 fits the door at the top of the stairs.

**The revelation.** They never left. Say it plainly through the environment, not a monologue: provisions still on the shelf with the receipts the party paid for, the hooded stranger's voice rising from the common room below, ordinary morning noise, and under it, pages turning.

**The choice.** Open the door and leave, or stay. Make staying a real temptation, not a trap: the Remnants are audible through the loft door, laughing, and the back room is warm.

- **Leave.** The job the stranger offered is real and ordinary. Each character permanently loses one misremembered thing (the detail that changed in Room 3 stays changed) and gains the level-up and a boon of the GM's choice tied to what they left behind in Room 3. The Remnants are gone; the players may say their first characters' names one last time if they want to.
- **Stay.** The campaign ends here, and it is allowed to be a good ending. Every session is session one. Tell the players that in those words.

**The twist for the GM only.** The back room is still there, under every tavern the party ever starts in. Any future campaign at this table can open with the barkeep setting out a tray.

## The Corridors (wandering between rooms)

The Corridors connect the five rooms and are where "stuck" happens. Three rules run them.

**1. No door leads back.** Every door the party uses is a Monk's tile that sends them to a random Corridor scene from the pool, never to the scene they left. The one exception is scripted: the Room 4 exit. In Foundry, keep one scene per Corridor and let the trigger pick from a weighted list.

**2. Something is in here with you — by visit count.** Track how many Corridor scenes the party has entered in total.

| Visits | Cue | Library layer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1–2 | Nothing. Furniture is wrong by one detail. | A strip of green wainscoting on one wall. |
| 3–4 | Sound: footsteps matching theirs, a half-beat late. A chair creaks in an empty room. (The Cover is reached from a pool door in this band or the next.) | Doors become double wooden doors with glass panels; through one, bookshelves. |
| 5–6 | Glimpse: a figure at the end of the corridor, gone when looked at directly. It's wearing something one of the players' first characters wore. | A pointed arch; a stained-glass lantern-window on a landing; carpet on stone. |
| 7+ | A Remnant is in the room. It's glad to see you. It doesn't attack. | Timber ribs overhead. The room is half library now. |

The library column is also how the scene copies differ (see "Running it in Foundry"): copy B of every place is one step more library than copy A, copy C one more. The changed detail has a direction.

**3. Rest costs memory, not hit points.** Short rests work normally. But each short rest taken in a Corridor rewrites one small thing about one character — a sibling's name, the colour of the house they grew up in, which god they swore by. The GM changes it on the sheet and tells only the other players. (This replaces the other AI's exhaustion tax: a timer that costs identity instead of actions.)

**Corridor pool (d8).** Eight in-between places, one misremembered trope each: stairwell, stable, hallway of inn doors, campfire clearing, market row at closing, ferry crossing, crossroads at dusk, cellar (from visit 5 only). The full specification — trope, wrongness, map, entry doors, library steps per copy, cues per visit band, dressing line — is **`corridor-pool.md`**, which wins over anything here. Never two tropes per room: one is uncanny, two is a joke. Every book anywhere in the Corridors is a half-page book.

## Remnants

**GM prep (the one thing to do before session one).** Privately ask each player: *What was the first D&D character you ever played? Name, class, and the one thing everyone remembers about them.* Don't say why. Players with no answer get a fallback Remnant below.

**Building a Remnant.**

- Take the first character's class and build it at the party's level using the standard NPC-building approach (or a sidekick-style stat block, if you'd rather keep it light).
- **Max hit points.** No rolling.
- **The Signature.** The one remembered thing becomes a feature that *always works*: once per round, it auto-succeeds, no roll, no save. The barbarian who was "just really strong" always hits. The rogue who always rolled nat 20s on Stealth is simply not there until it chooses to be.
- **Everything else is blank.** The Remnant cannot answer any question about its past that isn't the Signature. It doesn't know its hometown. It knows it had a tragic backstory but not what it was.
- **It never attacks first.** It wants the game to continue; it will fight if that's what the party wants to do.
- **It stands back up.** At 0 HP it rises at the start of its next turn, in the pose it fell in, with full HP, and continues mid-sentence.
- **It cannot leave a room through a door the party came through.** That's the only hard limit on it, and it's the exit mechanic for Room 4.

**Fallback Remnants (for players without an answer).** Three stock first-characters, built from the tropes:

| Remnant | Signature | The blank |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Rogue With a Tragic Past | Sneak Attack always applies, even in plain sight | Cannot say what the tragedy was. Gets upset when asked. |
| The Barbarian Who Is Just Strong | Every Strength check is an automatic success | Has no name. Answers to any name given, and keeps it. |
| The Wizard Who Misread the Spell | One fireball, always hits every target, cast at will | Has no other spells prepared and insists it does. |

**Using them.** One Remnant appears in Room 2. One more surfaces in the Corridors at visit 7+. All of them are in Room 4. Never stat them as enemies in the journal; file them under NPCs.

## Running it in Foundry

### Maps

Decided: all maps are built in **Dungeondraft** through the Dungeondraft MCP bridge, exported as Universal VTT (.dd2vtt) and converted into the module by `tools/import_dd2vtt.py`. The module ships its own images, walls, doors and lights; no importer module is needed at the table. (The earlier comparison of Dungeon Alchemist, Myxelium's importer and map packs is superseded; the Universal Battlemap Importer in particular fails to write images on v14.)

### Concealing repetition

Foundry gives repetition away three ways: explored fog (greyed areas the party has seen), the scene itself (same art, same walls, scene names in the navigation bar) and token placement (arriving on the square they left). A concealed loop has to beat all three. The techniques:

- **Fog reset.** A Monk's tile fires "reset fog" on scene entry, so a revisited scene looks unexplored. Blunt: it also erases what was explored a minute ago.
- **Scene copies.** A place the party should re-enter "fresh" exists two or three times as separate scenes — same map, one detail different — hidden from the navigation bar and named blandly ("Corridor"). Arriving in a copy, there is no fog to reset. One Dungeondraft map, imported under several keys.
- **Repeated geometry in one map.** Room 3's loop is not a teleport back to the start. The corridor is a strip of six to eight identical segments on one map and the party walks forward. Explored fog behind them is what a real corridor looks like. Each far door is a tile that lets them into the next identical segment or, once the loop is broken, into the exit. The engine shows nothing; attentive players see the scratch at the same height and the dropped coin back on the floor.
- **Different entry door.** Pool scenes have two or three entry doors. Arriving at a different one, with fog reset, reads as a new place.
- **Small vision.** Darkness up, light sources few, so each visit reveals a pool of lantern light rather than the room.

Which to use is a choice per transition:

| Repetition | Where | Technique |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Concealed — players must deduce it | Room 3's loop; Corridor visits 1–4 | Repeated segments; scene copies with one changed detail; fog reset; different entry door |
| Ambiguous — they suspect | Corridor visits 5–6; the bedroll still warm | Same scene, fog reset, but the detail that changed last time is still changed |
| Obvious — the reveal | Waking back in Room 1; the cellar from the wrong angle; Room 5 | Same scene, fog kept, same entry square. Let Foundry tell them |

The ambiguous row is where the "one detail changed" rule does its work: fog can't tell them they've been here; the half-page book on a different landing can.

### Scene structure

- The Corridor pool is eight *places*, each with two or three scene copies: about twenty Foundry scenes from eight Dungeondraft maps. Plus `cover-cavern`, one scene, one visit, reached by a scripted door at the GM's chosen visit (3–6). The random-door tile picks from the pool of copies, excluding the copy just left but allowing the same place's other copies.
- Room 3 is one long strip map (see above). Room 5 reuses the Room 1 scene with fog kept.
- A GM-only control tile, **Reveal**, restores fog and un-hides the copies' real names, for the moment in Room 5 when the players should see the whole shape of it.

### Automation (Monk's Active Tile Triggers)

Door → random pool copy (never the one left); visit counter incremented on every Corridor entry, driving the cue table; X door appearing when the previous room's condition is met; Room 3 segment doors and loop-break; Room 4 exit; Reveal. The v1 module's teleport, loop and lights-out tiles are the primitives.
